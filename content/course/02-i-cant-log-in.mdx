## The ticket

Three tickets arrive in the same hour. All three say the same thing.

```
Subject: can't log in
From: Agent, Acme Corp

I can't get in. It just says forbidden. I've tried three times.
```

```
Subject: login broken??
From: Agent, Globex

I'm logged in fine but when I open the Acme workspace link my
manager sent me it says "Not found". Is the site down?
```

```
Subject: locked out
From: Owner, Acme Corp

It keeps telling me to try again later. I KNOW my password is right.
```

**What you can tell from these alone:** that "I can't log in" isn't one
problem. It's a phrase customers use for at least four different ones:

- the system doesn't know who they are,
- it knows who they are and won't let them do something,
- it's hiding a page they're not allowed to know exists,
- or it's making them wait.

Each has a different cause, a different fix, and a different status code. By
the end of this lesson you'll have built all four answers into Deskly, broken
each one on purpose, and you'll be able to tell them apart from a screenshot.

## Why this part of the system exists

At the end of [Lesson 1](01-whose-data-is-this.mdx), Deskly had a serious
hole: anyone could read any company's tickets just by typing its name into
the address. The wall kept companies apart in the code, but it never asked
*who's asking*.

Think of an **office building** again. Lesson 1 gave every company its own
floor. This lesson adds the front desk:

1. **Reception asks for ID.** "Who are you?" No ID, no entry. That's
   **authentication**.
2. **Your badge only opens your company's floor.** You can be a real,
   checked-in visitor and still not be allowed onto Globex's floor. That's
   **authorization**.
3. **Inside your floor, your job decides which rooms you can enter.** The
   intern can read the files but not sign the contracts. That's **roles**.
4. **Reception slows down anyone who keeps guessing badge numbers.** That's
   **rate limiting**.

Every "I can't log in" ticket is a problem at one of those four steps. The
skill this lesson teaches is working out *which* step, quickly.

## Concepts you need

- **Authentication (AuthN)**: working out *who* is making a request. "Show
  me your ID."
- **Authorization (AuthZ)**: deciding what that person is *allowed* to do.
  "Your ID is fine, but this isn't your floor."
- **Password hash**: a scrambled fingerprint of a password that can't be
  turned back into the password. Deskly stores the fingerprint, never the
  password. At login it fingerprints what you typed and compares.
- **Salt**: a random string mixed in before hashing, different for every
  user. Two people with the same password get different fingerprints.
- **Cookie**: a small piece of text a website asks your browser to keep and
  send back on every visit to that site.
- **Session**: the coat-check model of login. At login, the server writes a
  note ("ticket `olr9…` belongs to adam") in its database and gives your
  browser the ticket number as a cookie. Your browser shows the ticket on
  every request; the server looks up the note.
- **Status code**: the three-digit number at the start of every web response.
  `200` means OK. The 400s mean "the problem is on your side". This lesson is
  mostly about four of them: `401`, `403`, `404` and `429`.
- **CSRF (cross-site request forgery)**: a trick where another website makes
  *your* browser send a request to a site you're logged into. The browser
  attaches your cookie automatically, so the request looks like you.
- **Token (JWT)**: the wristband model of login. Instead of a note on the
  server, the server hands out a signed pass that says "this is adam, valid
  for 15 minutes". Scripts use these instead of cookies.
- **OAuth**: one site trusting another to say who someone is. "Sign in with
  Google": you prove who you are *to Google*, and Google vouches for you to
  Deskly. Deskly never sees your Google password.
- **Rate limiting (throttling)**: a limit on how often someone can do
  something, like "5 login attempts a minute".

## Build it

This lesson builds on [Lesson 1](01-whose-data-is-this.mdx): Deskly running in
Docker, with two companies (Acme and Globex) and their tickets. The build
happens in six slices. The code lives in a new app called `accounts`
(Django's word for a folder of related code), plus a small file of access
rules in `organizations`.

### Slice 1: a real login

First, people. The seed command from Lesson 1 now also creates four demo
users, each one there to test one rule later:

| User | Company | Role | Here to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| olivia | Acme | owner | owners can do everything |
| adam | Acme | agent | agents can create tickets |
| vera | Acme | viewer | viewers can read but not write |
| gary | Globex | agent | he can't see Acme at all |

All four share the password `deskly-demo`. Passwords are set with
`set_password()`, which stores the salted fingerprint, never the password:

```python
user, _ = User.objects.get_or_create(username=username)
if not user.check_password(DEMO_PASSWORD):
    user.set_password(DEMO_PASSWORD)
    user.save()
```

The `if` line looks fussy. It's there because of a real bug, which is
[Break 1](#break-1-i-keep-getting-logged-out).

Then two endpoints, in `backend/apps/accounts/views.py`. The login:

```python
@api_view(["POST"])
@authentication_classes([])
@permission_classes([AllowAny])
@throttle_classes([LoginRateThrottle])
def login_view(request):
    user = authenticate(
        request,
        username=request.data.get("username"),
        password=request.data.get("password"),
    )
    if user is None:
        return Response(
            {"detail": "Invalid username or password."},
            status=status.HTTP_400_BAD_REQUEST,
        )
    login(request, user)
    return Response({"username": user.username})
```

(The `@authentication_classes` and `@throttle_classes` lines arrive in Slice 5.
They're shown here so you see the final version once.)

- **`authenticate()`** fingerprints the password and compares. It returns the
  user, or `None`.
- **`login()`** writes the coat-check note to the `django_session` table and
  tells the browser to keep a cookie called `sessionid`.
- **One vague error for both mistakes.** "Invalid username or password" never
  says *which* half was wrong. "No such user" would let anyone find out which
  usernames exist.

And "who am I?", which the React app calls when it opens:

```python
@api_view(["GET"])
@permission_classes([IsAuthenticated])
def me(request):
    memberships = request.user.memberships.select_related("organization")
    return Response({
        "username": request.user.username,
        "memberships": [
            {"org": m.organization.slug, "role": m.role}
            for m in memberships
        ],
    })
```

`IsAuthenticated` means "refuse anyone who isn't logged in". This one
endpoint is the most useful tool support has: it tells you who the system
thinks you are, and where you belong.

The session cookie is `HttpOnly`, which means JavaScript on the page can't
read it. If an attacker ever gets their own code onto a Deskly page, it still
can't steal your login.

### Slice 2: members only

Now the hole from Lesson 1. Every request to a company's address has to
answer a second question: "does *this person* belong to *this company*?"

In `backend/apps/organizations/access.py`:

```python
def get_membership_or_404(request, slug):
    """Return the user's membership in this org, or 404 if they have none."""
    return get_object_or_404(
        Membership.objects.select_related("organization"),
        organization__slug=slug,
        user=request.user,
    )
```

One lookup answers both questions: find a membership row for **this company
and this person**. No row means `404 Not Found`. Every company view now
starts with it:

```python
@api_view(["GET", "POST"])
@permission_classes([IsAuthenticated])
def ticket_list(request, slug):
    membership = get_membership_or_404(request, slug)
    organization = membership.organization
    ...
```

**Why `404` and not `403 Forbidden`?** `403` would mean "this company exists,
but you can't see it". Then anyone could type in company names (`tesla`,
`stripe`) and learn Deskly's customer list from the answers. With `404`, a
company you don't belong to and a company that doesn't exist look exactly the
same. A test compares the two responses to keep it that way.

This is the second ticket from the top of the lesson. Gary isn't broken. He's
at Globex, and the link he was sent is for Acme.

### Slice 3: roles

Belonging to a company isn't the end of it. Vera is a **viewer**: she can read
tickets, but she shouldn't be able to create them.

```python
WRITE_ROLES = {Membership.Role.OWNER, Membership.Role.AGENT}

def require_write_role(membership):
    """Stop viewers from changing anything. Raises 403."""
    if membership.role not in WRITE_ROLES:
        raise PermissionDenied("Viewers can read tickets but not change them.")
```

`ticket_list` calls it on a write, **before** anything is saved:

```python
    if request.method == "POST":
        require_write_role(membership)
        serializer = TicketSerializer(data=request.data)
        serializer.is_valid(raise_exception=True)
        serializer.save(organization=organization)
```

Two details matter:

- **The message says why.** "Viewers can read tickets but not change them"
  turns the ticket "it just says forbidden" into a one-line answer.
- **"Before anything is saved" is the whole point.** See
  [Break 2](#break-2-a-no-that-still-said-yes).

The checks now run in a fixed order, and each one has its own answer:

```
logged in?          no  → 401  "who are you?"
member of company?  no  → 404  "there's nothing here for you"
role allowed?       no  → 403  "I know you, and no"
                    yes → 200 / 201
```

A stranger never gets as far as the role check, so they learn nothing about
who has which job inside a company.

### Slice 4: CSRF, so other websites can't use your login

Here's the catch with cookies. Adam is logged into Deskly in one tab. In
another tab he opens a funny-cat website. Hidden in that page is a form that
quietly sends:

```
POST http://deskly/api/auth/logout/
```

**The browser attaches Adam's Deskly cookie automatically**, because that's
what cookies do. Deskly sees a valid login and logs him out. Swap "log out"
for "delete a ticket" and it stops being funny.

The cookie proves which **browser** sent the request. It doesn't prove which
**website** told the browser to send it. CSRF protection adds that missing
proof, with two checks Django makes on every logged-in write:

| Check | Question | Why the cat website fails it |
|---|---|---|
| **Token** | Did the request carry the secret `csrftoken` value in an `X-CSRFToken` header? | One website can't read another website's cookies, so it can't copy the value |
| **Origin** | Does the browser's `Origin` label match Deskly's own address? | The browser labels the request with the cat website's address, and the site can't change that label |

The cookie also has `SameSite=Lax`, which tells modern browsers not to send it
on most requests started by other sites at all. Three locks; if one fails, the
others hold.

Two new endpoints make it work. `GET /api/auth/csrf/` hands the browser the
token, and `POST /api/auth/logout/` ends the session. Logout is a `POST`
because it changes something. As a `GET`, any site could log you out with an
image tag.

On the React side, every write goes through one function, in
`frontend/src/api.ts`:

```ts
// Send data to Deskly. Every write carries the CSRF token.
export function postJson(path: string, data?: object) {
  return fetch(path, {
    method: "POST",
    headers: {
      "Content-Type": "application/json",
      "X-CSRFToken": getCookie("csrftoken"),
    },
    body: JSON.stringify(data ?? {}),
  });
}
```

One place sends the token, so no button can forget it.

### Slice 5: tokens for scripts, and a limit on guessing

**Tokens.** A script, such as a nightly report, can't easily juggle cookies and
CSRF tokens. So it gets a **JWT** instead, from a library called SimpleJWT:

```bash
curl -s -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"username": "adam", "password": "deskly-demo"}' \
  http://localhost:8000/api/auth/token/
# {"refresh":"eyJ...","access":"eyJ..."}

curl -H "Authorization: Bearer <access>" http://localhost:8000/api/me/
```

The **access** token lasts 15 minutes and goes on every request. The
**refresh** token lasts a day and is used only to get a new access token. The
browser never touches these: it keeps using its cookie, so there's no token
lying around in the page for injected code to steal.

Adding JWT changed one other thing. In `settings.py`, the list of ways to log
in is now explicit:

```python
"DEFAULT_AUTHENTICATION_CLASSES": [
    "rest_framework_simplejwt.authentication.JWTAuthentication",   # checked 1st
    "rest_framework.authentication.SessionAuthentication",         # checked 2nd
],
```

When nobody is logged in, Django REST Framework asks the **first** method in
the list what to say. Sessions can't tell a caller *how* to log in, so the
answer used to be `403`, which is misleading. JWT can ("send a Bearer token"),
so now it's a correct `401`:

```
HTTP/1.1 401 Unauthorized
WWW-Authenticate: Bearer realm="api"
{"detail":"Authentication credentials were not provided."}
```

"First" here doesn't mean "main". The browser sends no Bearer header, so it
skips straight past JWT and logs in with its cookie as before.

**Rate limiting.** Without a limit, anyone can try a million passwords a
minute against `/api/auth/login/`. That's called **brute force** (guessing),
or **credential stuffing** (trying passwords leaked from other sites). Both
password endpoints now share one limit:

```python
# backend/apps/accounts/throttles.py
class LoginRateThrottle(AnonRateThrottle):
    scope = "login"
```

```python
# settings.py
"DEFAULT_THROTTLE_RATES": {
    "login": "5/minute",
},
```

The sixth try in a minute gets:

```
429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: 42
```

That's the third ticket from the top. The owner's password *is* right. She
tried too many times, and she has to wait for the number of seconds in
`Retry-After`.

The count has to live somewhere every server process can see, so Deskly keeps
it in **Redis** (the fast shared store from Lesson 0):

```python
CACHES = {
    "default": {
        "BACKEND": "django.core.cache.backends.redis.RedisCache",
        "LOCATION": REDIS_URL,
    }
}
```

Why that matters is [Break 4](#break-4-the-lockout-that-forgot).

### Slice 6: sign in with Google

The last slice lets people skip the password and use their Google account.
OAuth works like this:

```
1. You click "Sign in with Google" on Deskly
2. Deskly sends your browser to Google            "Deskly wants your name and email. Allow?"
3. You log in AT GOOGLE and click Allow           (Deskly never sees your Google password)
4. Google sends your browser back to Deskly       with a one-time code in the address
5. Deskly's server swaps the code for your email  (server to server, using a secret)
6. Deskly finds the user with that email          and logs them in with a normal session
```

Deskly uses a library, **django-allauth**, for all of this. OAuth is exactly
the kind of code where home-made versions get small, serious security bugs,
so nobody should write it from scratch.

Setting it up has a part outside the code. In the Google Cloud console you
register Deskly, and you get a **client ID** (public) and a **client secret**
(which goes in `.env` and never in git). You also list the **redirect URI**:
the exact address Google is allowed to send people back to in step 4.

```
http://localhost:5173/accounts/google/login/callback/
```

Google refuses any address that isn't on that list. That's a security check.
The address receives the one-time code, so if any address were allowed, an
attacker could make a login link that sends *your* code to *their* site.

Two decisions shape how it behaves:

- **Only existing users can sign in.** A stranger with a Google account would
  belong to no company and see nothing, so sign-up is closed:

  ```python
  class NoSignupAdapter(DefaultAccountAdapter):
      def is_open_for_signup(self, request):
          return False
  ```

  An unknown Google account gets a "Sign Up Closed" page. New people will join
  by invitation in a later lesson.
- **Google's email decides which Deskly user you are.** That's only safe
  because Google checks that you own an email before vouching for it. And as
  [Break 5](#break-5-google-worked-now-the-password-doesnt) shows, Deskly has
  to have checked it too.

The button in the React app is a real HTML form that `POST`s, with the CSRF
token in a hidden field. A plain link would let any website start a Google
login in your browser without you noticing, which is the CSRF problem from
Slice 4 again.

## Break it, then fix it

Five breaks. Every one of them happened for real while building this, and
every one of them is a support ticket someone, somewhere, is filing today.
Each time, reason from **what you can see** before reading the cause.

### Break 1: "I keep getting logged out"

**Break it.** In `seed_demo.py`, remove the `if` so the password is set on
every run:

```python
user.set_password(DEMO_PASSWORD)
user.save()
```

Log in as adam, check `/api/me/` says adam, then run `make seed`.

**What the customer sees:** they were working, and suddenly they're logged out.
They log in again, and later it happens again. "I keep getting logged out."

**What you see:** the same cookie that worked a minute ago now gets:

```
{"detail":"Authentication credentials were not provided."}
```

adam's password is still `deskly-demo`. Nobody touched his account.

**Reason it through:**

1. **Is the cookie still there?** Yes. The browser sent it. So the browser
   didn't lose the login: the server stopped accepting it.
2. **Does his password still work?** Yes. So nothing is wrong with the
   account itself.
3. **What happened at the moment it broke?** The seed ran. What does the seed
   do to users?

<Reveal label="Think it through first, then show the root cause">

**Root cause:** `set_password()` makes a **new** fingerprint every time, because
it mixes in a new random salt, even for the same password. Django keeps a
copy of the fingerprint inside each session. When the stored one changes,
Django assumes the password was changed, and a password change logs you out
everywhere. That's a safety feature: if your password was stolen and you
change it, the thief's session dies too.

</Reveal>

**Fix it:** put the `if not user.check_password(...)` back. Now the seed only
touches a password that's actually wrong. Reseed, and adam stays logged in.

**Why this failure mode is dangerous:** the user did nothing, and the cause is
somewhere they can't see: a script, an import or a migration that rewrites the
password column. "I keep getting logged out" tickets often point at a
scheduled job, not the person.

### Break 2: a "no" that still said yes

**Break it.** In `ticket_list`, move `require_write_role(membership)` **below**
`serializer.save(...)`. Then send vera's write from the Verify section again.

**What the customer sees:** vera clicks "create ticket", gets an error that
says viewers can't create tickets, shrugs, and moves on.

**What you see:**

```
HTTP/1.1 403 Forbidden
{"detail":"Viewers can read tickets but not change them."}
```

It looks like the rule works. Now look at Acme's ticket list.

<Reveal label="Think it through first, then show the root cause">

**Root cause:** the ticket was saved, and *then* the code said no. "Vera was
here" is in Acme's list. The response tells you what the server *said*. It
tells you nothing about what the server already *did*.

</Reveal>

**Fix it:** the check goes back above the save. The test for this rule checks
**both** things: the `403`, and that no ticket exists afterwards. A test that
only checked the status code would have passed with the bug in place.

**Why this failure mode is dangerous:** the customer sees exactly the right
error. A support engineer who trusts the error closes the ticket. Meanwhile,
the data says otherwise.

### Break 3: works with curl, fails in the browser

This one wasn't planned. It happened.

**What the customer sees:** in the React app, login works. Then they click
**Log out** and get, in red:

```
CSRF Failed: Origin checking failed - http://localhost:5173 does not match any trusted origins.
```

**What you see:** the exact same logout, sent with curl and the same token,
returns `204 No Content`. It works.

**Reason it through:**

1. **Same request, two results.** So the difference is in what's sent, not
   what the server does with it. What does a browser add that curl doesn't?
2. **Read the error.** It's not "token missing". The token passed. It's the
   *Origin* check that failed.
3. **Browsers add an `Origin` header to every write. curl doesn't.** Django
   compares it with the address the request was sent to (the **Host**). So
   what's the Host?

<Reveal label="Think it through first, then show the root cause">

**Root cause:** in development, the React app's server (Vite) passes `/api`
requests on to the backend, like a receptionist forwarding a call. It was set
to rewrite the Host to `backend:8000` on the way through. So Django saw a
request labelled "from `localhost:5173`" addressed to `backend:8000`, which is
exactly what a cross-site attack looks like.

</Reveal>

**Fix it:** one setting in `frontend/vite.config.ts`, `changeOrigin: false`,
so the forwarder keeps the browser's Host. That's also what a real production
proxy does, so development now behaves like production.

**Why this failure mode is dangerous:** every curl test passes. The bug only
exists in a real browser, which is where customers are.

### Break 4: the lockout that forgot

**Break it.** Before Redis was added, the throttle count lived in Django's
default cache. Send six wrong passwords (`429` on the sixth), then:

```bash
docker compose restart backend
```

Try again.

**What the customer sees:** nothing unusual. They were told to wait, and then
they weren't.

**What you see:** the lockout is gone. The next attempt gets `400`, not `429`.

**Reason it through:**

1. **What does a restart throw away?** Anything kept in the program's memory.
2. **So where was the count kept?**

<Reveal label="Think it through first, then show the root cause">

**Root cause:** with no cache configured, Django keeps the count in the memory
of one Python process. A restart starts a new process with empty memory. In
production it's worse: servers run several processes at once, each with its
own memory and its own count. With four processes, "5 a minute" really means
about 20.

</Reveal>

**Fix it:** point the cache at Redis, which every process shares and which
doesn't restart with the backend. Repeat the break: still `429` after the
restart.

**A second hole in the same limit.** While checking this, something worse
turned up. Logged in as one user, eight wrong passwords for *another* user
gave:

```
[400, 400, 400, 400, 400, 400, 400, 400]
```

Never `429`. The limit only counted people who **weren't** logged in, and an
attacker can simply log in to their own account first. The fix was one line
on the login view, `@authentication_classes([])`. It makes the view ignore the
caller's own login, so everyone is counted. The limit had looked at *who's
sending*, when the risk is *who's being guessed*.

**Why this failure mode is dangerous:** a limit always looks like it works when
you test it the obvious way. Ask where the counter lives, and **what it
actually counts**.

### Break 5: Google worked, now the password doesn't

**Break it.** In the Django shell, give olivia your Gmail address the
obvious way:

```python
olivia.email = "you@gmail.com"
olivia.save()
```

Sign in with Google. It works: you're olivia. Now log out and log in as olivia
with her password.

**What the customer sees:** "Google login works, but my password stopped
working."

**What you see:** the password login fails, and in the shell:

```python
>>> olivia.has_usable_password()
False
```

Her stored password now starts with `!`, which is Django's way of writing "no
password can ever match this".

**Reason it through:**

1. **Nobody changed her password by hand.** So something did it automatically,
   and it happened during the Google login.
2. **Why would a login library *destroy* a password on purpose?** Think about
   who might have set that password.

<Reveal label="Think it through first, then show the root cause">

**Root cause:** allauth did it deliberately. Imagine an attacker signs up
*before you*, using *your* email address, and keeps the password. Nobody checks
the email. Later you sign in with Google, Google confirms the email is yours,
and you're logged into the attacker's account. Now you both have it. So when
the Google email matches a user whose email Deskly never **verified**, allauth
wipes the old password to lock that person out. Setting `olivia.email` by hand
doesn't count as verified.

</Reveal>

**Fix it:** run `make seed` to restore the password, then record the email as
verified in allauth's own email table:

```python
from allauth.account.models import EmailAddress
EmailAddress.objects.update_or_create(
    user=olivia, email=olivia.email,
    defaults={"verified": True, "primary": True},
)
```

Sign in with Google again, and the password survives. In a real product,
nobody types this: an email becomes verified when the person clicks a link
sent to it, or accepts an invitation.

**Why this failure mode is dangerous:** it looks like a bug, and it's a
security feature. A support engineer who "fixes" it by resetting the password
and moving on hasn't found the real question: *was this email ever verified,
and who set that password?*

## Trade-offs & decisions

**Sessions for the browser, tokens for scripts.**

| Option | Good | Bad | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Session cookie** | The browser handles it. JavaScript can't read it. The server can end it instantly by deleting the note | The server stores a row per login. Writes need CSRF protection | ✅ the React app |
| **JWT** | No lookup on every request. Easy for scripts. No CSRF needed | Can't be cancelled before it expires. Dangerous if stored where page scripts can read it | ✅ scripts and API clients |

Many tutorials put a JWT in the browser's `localStorage`. Any script that gets
onto the page can read `localStorage`, so one injected script steals the login.
Deskly's browser login stays in an `HttpOnly` cookie for that reason.

**`404` for "not a member", not `403`.** It costs support a little: the status
alone doesn't say whether the person typed the wrong company or lacks a
membership. That answer lives in the database, where only support can see it,
not in a response anyone can read. That's where it belongs.

**`401` vs `403`, decided by order.** The status for "nobody logged in" comes
from whichever login method is listed first. Putting JWT first made it the
correct `401`. It's a small setting with a big effect on support: a `403` that
really meant "not logged in" sends people checking permissions instead of
logging in.

**A plain function for roles, not a permission class.** Django REST Framework
has "permission classes" that run before a view. They run before the
membership is looked up, so they'd have to look it up a second time. A
function called inside the view reuses the membership Slice 2 already found.
That's fine for a handful of views. It gets revisited when there are many.

**Only existing users can use Google.** Opening sign-up would create users who
belong to nothing, and email matching would need much more care. Closed
sign-up plus invitations (later) is simpler and safer.

## What actually broke for me

Real errors from building this, quoted exactly as they appeared. Breaks 1, 3, 4
and 5 above were all real too.

**One capital letter took down every page.**

```
curl: (56) Recv failure: Connection reset by peer
```

Not an error page: the server hung up. The logs explained:

```
ImportError: cannot import name 'isAuthenticated' from 'rest_framework.permissions' (/usr/local/lib/python3.12/site-packages/rest_framework/permissions.py). Did you mean: 'IsAuthenticated'?
```

Python names are case-sensitive, and Django loads every view file at startup,
so one bad import stops everything. **When the server hangs up, read the logs,
not another curl.**

**`403` that meant "not logged in".** Before Slice 5, calling `/api/me/` with no
cookie gave:

```
HTTP/1.1 403 Forbidden
{"detail":"Authentication credentials were not provided."}
```

The number said "forbidden", and the body said "I don't know who you are". The
body was right. **Read the body, not just the number.**

**New URLs that didn't exist.** The CSRF and logout endpoints went into the
`organizations` app by mistake, and gave Django's "Page not found (404)". Every
address in that app starts with `/api/orgs/`, and its `<slug>/` pattern would
have treated `auth` as a company name anyway. **A `404` on a URL you just added:
check which file it went into.**

**Google said no.** Starting the Google login from the backend's own address
(port 8000) instead of the React app (5173):

```
You can't sign in because this app sent an invalid request. You can try again later, or contact the developer about this issue.
Error 400: redirect_uri_mismatch
```

My first guess was "the backend doesn't know how to log in with Google". It
did: the browser had reached Google, and Google refused the return address,
which said 8000 when only 5173 was registered. **Compare the `redirect_uri` in
the error details with the list in the console.** It's nearly always a port,
`http` vs `https`, `www.`, or a trailing slash.

**Logged in, then a 404.** After choosing a Google account:

```
The current path, accounts/profile/, didn’t match any of these.
```

The login had worked. Django sends people to `/accounts/profile/` after login
unless told otherwise, and the setting that says "go back to the app" was
missing. **"I logged in and got a 404": check whether they're logged in before
debugging the login.**

## Production reality check

What this lab does vs. what a real SaaS company would do. The full list is in
[`docs/known-gaps.md`](../../docs/known-gaps.md).

- ✅ **Closed: anyone could read any company's data.** The 🔴 gap from Lesson 1
  is fixed by Slice 2, with a test per endpoint. (GAP-006)
- 🟠 **Login cookies can travel over plain HTTP.** Fine on `localhost`. In
  production, cookies get the `Secure` flag and everything runs over HTTPS, or
  someone on the same café Wi-Fi can copy a cookie and *be* that user.
  (GAP-013)
- 🟡 **The login form itself isn't CSRF-protected.** A malicious site could
  log your browser into *its* account, and you might then type private
  details into the wrong place. `SameSite` blunts most of this. (GAP-014)
- 🟠 **The login limit counts per internet address only.** A whole office
  behind one address gets locked out together, and an attacker with many
  addresses gets around it. Production adds a second limit per username.
  (GAP-015)
- 🟡 **JWTs can't be cancelled.** A stolen access token works until it expires
  (15 minutes), and a stolen refresh token for a day. Production keeps a
  blocklist of cancelled tokens. (GAP-016)
- 🟡 **allauth brought a second password login page** (`/accounts/login/`) with
  its own rules. Production picks one front door. (GAP-017)

## Support takeaways

**"I can't log in": find out which step failed. The status code tells you.**

| They see | Code | It means | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Invalid username or password" | `400` | The login attempt failed | Check the username, then caps lock. Offer a reset |
| "Credentials were not provided" | `401` | The system doesn't know who they are | Log in again: session expired, cookie blocked, or token expired |
| "Not found" on a company page | `404` | Not a member of that company (or it doesn't exist) | Check their memberships. Wrong workspace link? |
| "Forbidden" with a reason | `403` | Logged in, member, but their role can't do this | Check their role. A viewer trying to write? |
| "CSRF Failed: …" | `403` | The page's safety token or origin check failed | Reload the page. If it keeps happening, escalate: a proxy or setting is wrong |
| "Too many requests" | `429` | Too many tries in a short time | Wait for `Retry-After`. A whole office at once? Shared address |
| Google: `redirect_uri_mismatch` | Google's `400` | The app's Google settings don't match its address | Escalate: a configuration problem, not the user's |

**Next time, check in this order:**

1. **Ask for the exact message, or a screenshot.** "It says forbidden" and "it
   says not found" are different tickets. The words matter more than the
   customer's summary.
2. **Ask what they were trying to do.** Logging in, opening a page, or saving
   something? A failure only on save points at their role.
3. **Look them up.** Which companies do they belong to, with which role?
   That's what `/api/me/` returns for them, or the memberships table for you.
4. **Ask what changed.** A new device, a password change, an admin script, a
   new office? "I keep getting logged out" often comes from something the user
   never touched.

**Questions to ask the customer:** What exact message do you see? Which
workspace, or what address, were you on? Were you logging in, viewing, or
saving? Are colleagues in the same office having the same problem? Did you
sign in with a password or with Google?

**When to escalate:**

- **Anything with "CSRF"** that a page reload doesn't fix.
- **`redirect_uri_mismatch`** or any error on Google's own page. The
  configuration is wrong, and no user action fixes it.
- **"Google works but my password doesn't"** on an account nobody can explain.
  It may mean someone else set that password.
- **A whole office locked out at once.** One person can't cause that, unless
  they all share one address.
